Beyond the resource curse: what lets some economies climb the value ladder

A theory-driven regression and a data-driven machine-learning ensemble are pointed at the same panel of 54 resource-rich economies. They converge on three pillars — finance, infrastructure, and human capital — and the case studies show which one binds where.

Ayaan Tigdikar March 2026 10 min read | Methods: panel econometrics · machine learning

Adapted from an LSE Capstone project undertaken for Moody's, 2026.

What the analysis finds

  • Across 54 resource-rich economies from 1995 to 2019, three structural pillars — financial depth (domestic credit to the private sector), infrastructure (electricity access), and human capital — are the most robust correlates of economic complexity.
  • Human capital matters most where extraction is most intense: the interaction of schooling with resource production is a top predictor across every model.
  • Traditional macro and institutional variables — trade openness, political stability — add little once these pillars and a country's own complexity history are accounted for.
  • Which constraint binds shifts with development: infrastructure in Congo, the political management of the rent in Azerbaijan, and the depth of skills and R&D in Chile.

Natural resources were supposed to be a shortcut. A country sitting on oil, copper or gas has, in principle, a ready-made source of foreign exchange and public revenue with which to build everything else. Yet for much of the developing world the shortcut never opened onto anywhere: export baskets stayed narrow, manufacturing stayed thin, and the windfall funded consumption rather than capability. Chile and Malaysia show it can be done. They are the exceptions that make the rule visible.

The most influential framing of that failure is the resource curse — the idea that resource endowments deterministically depress long-run growth, whether through currency appreciation that hollows out manufacturing (the "Dutch disease") or through the politics of rent-seeking and weak institutions. But the empirical case has eroded. The original cross-country result rested on treating resource exports as if they were exogenous, when they plainly depend on investment, institutions and infrastructure. Once that endogeneity is taken seriously, much of the curse dissolves. The interesting question is no longer whether resources hurt, but under what conditions a country converts extraction into broader productive capability — the ability to make complex, high-value things.

To measure that capability we use the Economic Complexity Index (ECI), which infers the sophistication of an economy from the mix of products it exports competitively, across a panel of 54 resource-dependent developing economies observed annually from 1995 to 2019. We then point two deliberately different lenses at the same data: a theory-driven panel regression, and a data-driven machine-learning ensemble. Where both agree, we can be more confident the finding is real rather than an artefact of one modelling choice.

What theory predicts

The first lens is a panel regression grounded in intertemporal investment theory. It puts complexity on the left and a pre-specified set of regressors on the right, with a lagged dependent variable to capture the strong persistence of capability and to blunt the most obvious sources of endogeneity. Choosing the specification in advance guards against fishing for significance; the cost is that anything the theory omits, the model cannot see.

Two variables come through clean. The Human Capital Index and gross fixed capital formation enter positively and significantly in every specification. Resource-production intensity is negatively signed — the direction the curse would predict — but never statistically significant once other factors are present. And the institutional variables that dominate so much of the growth literature — rule of law, political stability, trade openness — wash out once a country's own complexity history is controlled for. Within countries, over this window, those things simply move too little to explain the variation.

What the data say on their own

The second lens abandons the theory. Regularised regressions — LASSO, Ridge and Elastic Net — and a Random Forest are handed a broad menu of candidate predictors and left to decide which earn their keep, shrinking weak signals toward zero and averaging away noise. If the theory-first model missed something that matters empirically, this is where it should surface.

The consensus is striking. Domestic credit to the private sector — a proxy for financial development that never appeared in the econometric specification — emerges as the single strongest predictor. Human capital and its interaction with resource production, and electricity access, follow. The Random Forest, which makes no linearity assumptions at all, tells the same story: once the lagged level of complexity is set aside, the same handful of structural variables rise to the top.

Domestic credit, electricity, and human capital that pays off precisely where extraction is most intense — the same three pillars survive whether the model is built from theory or from the data.

That last clause is the most policy-relevant result. Human capital is not uniformly useful: its return to complexity is largest exactly where a country is most resource-dependent — where the distance between what the economy currently makes and the capabilities it would need to diversify is widest. Schooling and skills are the bridge across that distance.

For the record, the models predict complexity levels well — test R² around 0.85 — but year-on-year changes poorly, with R² below 0.15. Capability is sticky; where a country will be next year is mostly where it is now. That persistence is a caution against over-reading any single year, and it sets up the starkly uneven long-run record.

Climbers and stragglers

Over the quarter-century, the spread of outcomes is enormous. Papua New Guinea, Vietnam and Bahrain registered the largest gains in complexity; Venezuela, Chad and Libya the largest losses — in several cases collapsing from the middle of the global distribution to its bottom.

Where the constraint binds

Averages conceal mechanism. To see how the three pillars operate on the ground — and how the binding constraint shifts with the level of development — the analysis closes with three cases, chosen to sit at different rungs of the ladder: the Republic of the Congo, Azerbaijan, and Chile.

Republic of the Congo — the infrastructure floor

For an oil economy that jointly lacks financial depth, human capital and infrastructure, the binding constraint is the most basic one: power. Unreliable electricity and thin transport networks cap the non-oil economy before finance or skills can even become the marginal problem, while procyclical public spending amplifies every swing in the oil price. Azerbaijan — Dutch disease, institutionalised

Azerbaijan clears the infrastructure bar but runs into the classic trap. An oil boom that appreciated the real exchange rate and crowded out non-oil tradeables, layered over weak institutions and limited transparency, left the country's complexity falling across the period — one of the sharpest declines in the sample. Here the constraint is not a missing power station but the political economy of the rent itself.

Chile — the frontier problem

Chile is the region's success story, and precisely for that reason it isolates the hardest constraint. Strong institutions and solid infrastructure did not, on their own, arrest a gentle decline in complexity since the mid-2000s. What remains binding at Chile's level of development is the top of the ladder — human-capital depth and research-and-development capacity. Its mining sector is world-class and highly concentrated, as the next three views show, but concentration in extraction is not the same as capability in production.

The upshot

The resource curse, as a deterministic law, does not survive contact with this data. What survives is more useful: a short, consistent list of things that let resource-rich economies build capability — and a warning that the list is sequenced. Finance, power and human capital all matter, but at low incomes the constraint is infrastructure, in the middle it is the political management of the rent, and at the frontier it is the depth of skills and innovation. Policy that treats all three as interchangeable will spend on the wrong one.

The most actionable finding is the interaction. Human capital does the most work precisely where resource dependence is highest — which is to say, in exactly the economies that most need to diversify and are furthest from being able to. That is an argument for treating schooling and skills not as a social good to be afforded once growth arrives, but as the central instrument of diversification itself.
