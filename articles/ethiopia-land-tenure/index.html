The tax on leaving

Ethiopia is among the most rural economies in Africa, with one of the widest gaps between farm and city productivity. The obstacle to closing it is not a shortage of land rights — it is that those rights are tied to staying put. Tenure conditioned on use works as a hidden tax on migration.

Ayaan Tigdikar 2026 9 min read | Method: policy analysis

Adapted from a policy essay on land tenure and labour mobility in Ethiopia.

The argument in brief

  • Ethiopia keeps roughly 76% of its people rural and 63% of employment in agriculture, even though output per worker outside farming is more than four times higher — a large, unrealised gain from moving labour.
  • The country already has land certificates and legal rental rights. What it lacks is tenure secure enough to survive absence: keep your plot only if you keep working it.
  • That use-condition is an implicit tax on migration. The market it breaks is the rental market for land, not the migration market — and fixing one fixes both.
  • Evidence from Mexico and from Ethiopia itself supports the direction of reform, though the likely magnitude is modest and bounded by what a country that cannot fully privatise land can achieve.

Few development puzzles are as stark, or as old, as the gap between the farm and the city. In Ethiopia the gap is enormous: a worker outside agriculture produces more than four times what a worker inside it does. Standard economics says labour should flow toward the higher return until the gap closes. Yet roughly three in four Ethiopians still live in rural areas, and nearly two in three still work the land. The reallocation that ought to be happening is not happening.

And the returns to moving are not just theoretical. Tracking Ethiopian households that sent an internal migrant against those that did not, one study recovers consumption gains on the order of 165% for the migrant-sending households. If moving pays that well, why do so few move? The usual answer — that migration itself is somehow blocked — turns out to be the wrong place to look.

The roots of the divide run through the land. Ethiopia's regime is a direct legacy of the 1975 Derg reform, which nationalised all rural land and administered it through local peasant associations whose periodic redistributions left households perpetually unsure of holding on to their plots. The 2005 Land Act softened this: it curtailed the redistributions and launched a low-cost programme certifying households' usufruct rights — their right to use the land — and permitting them to rent it out. On paper, that is most of what a functioning land market needs.

The catch is in the fine print, which varies region by region. Some regions cap the share of a holding that may be rented out at a half; others limit contract length or require family consent and official approval. In Tigray, a holder absent from the village for more than two years risks losing the plot altogether. The rights exist — but they are conditional on staying and using the land.

The market that actually fails

Start from how the market should work. A household holding usufruct rights to a plot would rent or sell those rights whenever someone else valued the land more highly — that is, whenever another farmer's expected output on it exceeded the household's own. Land would flow from less to more productive cultivators, and the household's labour would be freed for its best alternative use, very often a wage job in the city. Everyone who transacts is better off; the planner is happy.

Ethiopia implements this logic only halfway. Certificates and rental rights exist, but the use-condition quietly rewrites the household's problem. If being absent raises the chance of losing the plot, then keeping the plot means keeping someone on it — so the household holds a member back on the farm even when that member would earn far more in town. The distance between the labour the household chooses and the labour it would choose under secure tenure is the implicit migration tax, made concrete.

The crucial move is to see which market is broken. It is not the market for migration — nothing stops a person from boarding a bus to Addis Ababa. It is the rental market for land. Regional rules choke the supply of rental contracts directly, through caps and consent requirements, and indirectly, by attaching the risk of losing the plot to being away. Because land cannot travel with its holder, a household that cannot rent out its plot cannot leave without abandoning its single largest asset. Relax the restriction on renting, and you relax the constraint on moving at the same time.

Nothing stops an Ethiopian from boarding a bus to the city. What stops them is that the land cannot come too — and cannot safely be left behind.

This matters most precisely because migration is risky. Take-up of migration stays low even where average returns are high, because the return is personal: a household only learns what moving pays them by trying it, and for a family near subsistence a failed trial can be catastrophic. A working rental market is exactly the insurance such a family needs — it turns the plot into a stream of income that can underwrite the gamble of leaving.

The clean test: Mexico's PROCEDE

The closest thing to a controlled experiment comes from Mexico. Between 1993 and 2006, the PROCEDE programme issued ownership certificates to some 3.6 million households in ejidos — communities whose members had held use-conditional rights of exactly the Ethiopian kind. Crucially, PROCEDE removed the cultivation requirement and legalised renting, selling and bequeathing the land: a near-clean test of delinking tenure from use.

The result matched the theory. Certification raised the probability that a household had a migrant member by 28%. Cultivated area did not fall — the plots left behind were folded into the holdings of those who stayed, exactly the consolidation the framework predicts — and non-food consumption rose, consistent with money being sent home from higher-wage destinations. Delinking released labour and reallocated land in a single move, with the rental market doing the work.

Two cautions travel with that number. Internally, the estimate leans on a staggered rollout with no never-treated group and a single certification dummy, which assumes the effect is the same across cohorts and over time — an assumption the authors' own figures strain, since the earliest-certified communities lost population faster than later ones. The authors defend the design with pre-trend and falsification tests, and largely succeed; but modern staggered difference-in-differences methods would tell the story more cleanly.

The bigger question is whether Mexico transfers to Ethiopia at all. PROCEDE went further than anything Ethiopia could do — it granted transferable ownership, full privatisation, which the Ethiopian constitution forbids. Mexican households were also better placed to use their freedom: income per head was roughly four times Ethiopia's today, and NAFTA-era Mexico offered abundant high-wage destinations at home and across the US border, where Ethiopia's cities can absorb far less. The 28% is best read as an upper bound on what an Ethiopian reform could achieve.

Ethiopia's own evidence

Closer to home, a study of 1,170 rural Ethiopian youth across 27 kebeles in Amhara and Oromia asks a neat question: does expecting to inherit more land change whether young people leave? Using an instrumental variable built from the timing of past land redistributions, it finds that a 10% increase in expected inheritance cuts long-distance permanent migration by 8.1 percentage points and rural-to-urban migration by 4.8, while raising agricultural employment by 6.2 — young people with more land coming to them stay, and farm.

The instrument is carefully built, with a strong first stage and a placebo test that behaves as it should, so the headline is credible. But the most policy-relevant part — that the pull of land is roughly twice as strong where rental markets are thin — rests on a simpler correlation, because the instrument is too weak to apply in sub-samples. And two gaps limit how far the result carries. The institutional setting it exploits has eroded since 2014, as certification has strengthened, so today's effects are probably smaller. More fundamentally, the study measures the pull of inheriting land, which is not quite the same lever as strengthening the right to rent it out.

What it adds up to

The direction is clear; the magnitude is not. Both the Mexican experiment and Ethiopia's own data point the same way — use-conditional tenure holds labour on the farm, and loosening it would let some of that labour move to where it is worth more. What neither can pin down is how much. The Mexican figure is an upper bound, inflated by full privatisation and a richer economy with better destinations. Much of the easy gain in Ethiopia has arguably already been banked by the post-2005 reforms, leaving a smaller margin for further delinking to capture.

Two things could even shrink the prize. In general equilibrium, a wave of migrants into Addis Ababa would bid down urban wages and narrow the very gap the policy is chasing. And the accounting so far ignores what happens to those left behind — remittances and word of urban wages that might lift origin households and pull more migrants after them, set against the loss of the young and able whose departure could drag down what remains of farm productivity. The existing studies control for none of this, so the net welfare effect cannot simply be read off their estimates.

That points to where the research should go next: measuring misallocation directly, from what migrants actually earn at their destinations rather than from whether they moved; and mapping the general-equilibrium and spillover effects of moving people out of farming at scale. Until then, the honest conclusion is a qualified yes — delinking tenure from use is the right direction for Ethiopia, pursued in the knowledge that the gains are real but bounded.
