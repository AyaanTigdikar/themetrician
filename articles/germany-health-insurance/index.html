When the healthy opt out

Germany is one of the few countries that lets its highest earners opt out of public health insurance. The result is a slow, self-reinforcing drain of the young, wealthy and healthy — and a bill that falls on everyone left behind.

Ayaan Tigdikar20259 min read|Method: policy analysis

Adapted from an LSE health-economics essay.

The argument in brief

  • Since 1970 Germany has let workers earning above €73,800 leave the public system (GKV) for risk-priced private cover (PKV).
  • Because income and health move together, the opt-out pulls the wealthy and the healthy out of the shared pool — the members it can least afford to lose.
  • That triggers a spiral: a sicker pool, rising contributions (12.4% → 13.6% in the 1990s), and yet more healthy members leaving. Just 11% of private members are over 65, versus 22% in the public system.
  • The efficiency case for the opt-out is unproven — the promised gains from competition never arrived. The equity case against it is not: the cost falls on the poor and sick who cannot leave.

Most European welfare states run one public health system and make everyone join. The logic is unglamorous but powerful: put the whole population in a single pool and the healthy quietly subsidise the sick, the young the old, the lucky the unlucky. Germany does something stranger. Since 1970 it has let its highest earners walk out of the public system altogether and buy private cover instead — and that escape hatch is the source of a slow, self-reinforcing problem.

Germany's public scheme, the GKV, works on solidarity: you pay a flat share of your income — currently 14.6% — and everyone gets the same benefits, whatever their health. Its private alternative, the PKV, works on the opposite principle: premiums are priced to your individual risk — age, health, income. Anyone earning above a threshold (today €73,800 a year) may leave the public pool for the private one. The question this raises, and the one the underlying research asks, is simple to state: should Germany close the escape hatch?

The logic, in plain terms

Insurance only works because of pooling. In any group, a few people will run up large medical bills and most will not, but nobody knows in advance who. Everyone pays in; the pool pays out; the risk is shared. The catch is that people usually know their own health better than an insurer does. Give them the choice, and the healthy start to notice that they are paying more than they take out — that they are subsidising everyone else — and look for a better deal.

A compulsory public system fixes this by removing the choice: if everyone must join, the healthy cannot leave, and the pool stays broad. Germany's opt-out puts the choice back — but only for high earners. That would matter little if income and health were unrelated. They are not: richer people are, on average, healthier. So the people allowed to leave are exactly the ones the pool can least afford to lose — the wealthy and the well. In the private market their low risk earns them a low premium; in the public pool their income earned them a high contribution. They leave.

What they leave behind is a smaller, sicker, poorer pool. Its average cost rises, so its contributions must rise too — and here is the sting: those higher contributions fall not on the general taxpayer but on the people still inside. Which makes the public system a worse deal for the next-healthiest member, who now also has reason to leave. Round it goes.

Whether this is a bad thing for the country as a whole is genuinely ambiguous. Forcing everyone into one pool also has losers — healthy people who would rather not pay for cover they value less than it costs them. So the case for compulsion rests on a balance: the gains to those who get protected must outweigh the losses to those who are made to pay. The opt-out tilts that balance by pulling the cheapest members out, and it can only be worth it if the freedom it grants, plus whatever competition and innovation private insurers bring, is large enough to offset the damage to the shared pool. The equity side is far less ambiguous: the cost of the spiral lands squarely on the poor and the sick who cannot leave.

Who ends up where

The data show the sorting plainly. Private cover skews young, wealthy and healthy; the public pool carries the old and the ill. Just 11% of private members are over 65, against 22% in the public system — the retired, who use the most care, are twice as concentrated in the pool that cannot turn anyone away.

And the sorting is driven by health, not just price. Studies find that people who opt out are markedly less likely to be hospitalised the following year, and that relative prices explain only about a third of the selection — the rest is people acting on what they know about their own health. Private insurers reinforce it through “cream-skimming”: they can refuse or heavily surcharge high-risk applicants, who then fall back on the public system. And those who switch from private back to public tend to be the ones who have just suffered a health shock. Every one of these channels pushes risk in the same direction — toward the pool that must accept it.

The people allowed to leave are precisely the ones the public pool can least afford to lose: the wealthy, and the well.

The spiral in the numbers

Hard evidence on the efficiency cost is thinner than the evidence for selection itself, but the history is suggestive. Once high earners could freely leave, the public system's finances came under strain and its contribution rate climbed from 12.4% in 1991 to 13.6% in 1997 — and each rise, as the model predicts, gave more young and healthy members a reason to follow the others out. Rising contributions are not just an efficiency worry but an equity one: recent work finds they bite hardest on low-income households, squeezing their spending and their welfare.

Did competition deliver?

The whole efficiency defence of the opt-out rests on a promise: that private insurers, competing for customers, would drive innovation and force the public system to improve. Germany's own Council of Economic Experts has concluded that those gains never really materialised. If the competitive upside is largely absent, the ledger tilts: there is little efficiency benefit to weigh against the selection damage, and the case for keeping the escape hatch open weakens accordingly.

The verdict

On efficiency, honesty requires a shrug. The opt-out clearly feeds adverse selection and clearly raises costs in the public pool, but the net effect on the whole economy is hard to pin down, precisely because the promised gains from competition are so hard to find — and, on the evidence, so small. On equity, no shrug is needed. Letting the healthy and wealthy exit, and loading the cost of their departure onto the sick and poor who remain, is regressive by construction. It has helped produce exactly the two-tier system critics feared: faster, fuller care for those who can buy their way into the private tier, tightening constraints for those who cannot.

That points to a clear ordering of priorities. The efficiency case for the opt-out is, at best, unproven; the equity case against it is not. Whether the remedy is to close the escape hatch outright or to blunt it with stronger redistribution and tighter rules on private insurers, the reform that matters is the one that stops the German system from asking the people with the least to subsidise the exit of the people with the most.
