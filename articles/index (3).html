Style over substance

Automated fake-news detectors score near-perfect accuracy on standard benchmarks — and fall apart the moment the writing style is changed or the topic shifts. What they have really learned is which newswire an article came from.

Ayaan Tigdikar20269 min read|Methods: NLP · adversarial ML

Adapted from an LSE group project (ST456), 2026.

The argument in brief

  • Fake-news detectors reach 99%+ accuracy on the ISOT benchmark, but the benchmark is source-confounded — every real article is a Reuters wire story.
  • No architecture is robust to both attack types: BERT resists word swaps but collapses under style transfer; the CNN and BiLSTM do the reverse.
  • The models flip a real article to ‘fake’ far more easily than the reverse — they detect credible style, not fabrication.
  • Adversarial retraining fixes the attack it is shown but not the disease: accuracy on out-of-distribution news is barely above chance. The bottleneck is dataset construction.

There is a number that ought to make anyone suspicious. Modern fake-news detectors, fed a standard benchmark, routinely score above 99% accuracy — the kind of result that in most of machine learning signals either a triumph or a leak. Here it is the second. The models are not learning to spot misinformation. They are learning to spot the newswire it was scraped from.

The benchmark in question is ISOT, a corpus of about 39,000 articles after cleaning, with real news drawn from Reuters and fake news from a scattering of unreliable outlets. The tell is almost comically direct: every real article begins with the pattern “[city] (Reuters)”. A classifier does not need to understand truth to exploit that. It needs to recognise a dateline. Strip the obvious source tags and the lexical overlap between real and fake articles is large — enough that word content alone cannot separate them. Whatever the 99% is measuring, it is mostly the letterhead.

Two ways to fool a detector

To probe what the models had really learned, we attacked them in two escalating ways. The first, TextFooler, is a word-level attack: it swaps a handful of the most influential words for close synonyms, nudging the input without changing its meaning. The second is an article-level style transfer using an LLM — rewriting a real article in the sensational register of a tabloid, or a fake one in the measured tone of a wire service, while preserving every factual claim. Three architectures faced both: a CNN and a BiLSTM on GloVe embeddings, and a fine-tuned BERT as an upper bound.

The pattern in Figure 1 is the whole story in miniature. Against word-level swaps, BERT holds up best, because its self-attention re-reads every token in the context of the whole article and dilutes a lone substitution. Against a full change of style, BERT collapses hardest — falling from 0.99 to 0.31 — precisely because that same contextual machinery had learned to lean on register as its main signal. The CNN, which fixates on local word patterns, is the mirror image: easy to fool word-by-word, harder to fool by restyling. The uncomfortable conclusion is that robustness to one attack buys fragility to the other.

They detect credibility, not fabrication

Look at which direction the style attack works, and the models give themselves away. Degrading a genuine article into tabloid prose flips it to “fake” most of the time; dressing a fabricated article in credible prose flips it to “real” far less often. The models have learned that the presence of formal journalistic convention means “real” — not any positive marker of fabrication. Take the conventions away and they default to fake; add them and the residual signals of fabrication mostly survive.

Probing the CNN by iteratively hiding the words it depends on confirms the diagnosis. It leans first on named entities and wire-service furniture (“said”, standardised dates, “Reuters”), then, once those are masked, on sensational vocabulary (“crazy”, “hoax”, “disgusting”) as a proxy for the fake class, and finally on stop-words with no discriminative value at all. Nowhere in that hierarchy is there a representation of whether a claim is true.

The classifiers had not learned to detect misinformation. They had learned to detect Reuters — and anything that dressed like it.

Whack-a-mole with adversarial retraining

The natural fix is to show the models the attacks during training. It works — suspiciously well. After retraining on restyled examples, all three models recover to above 99.5% against the LLM attack, BERT to a perfect score. But the robustness is not free: BERT's resistance to the word-level attack actually falls (from 0.83 to 0.78), because suppressing the style shortcut pushed it back onto exactly the token-level cues TextFooler targets. Close one hole and another opens.

The collapse out of distribution

The real test is whether any of this transfers to news the models have never seen. Evaluated on FakeNewsAMT — a balanced set spanning six topics with no source overlap with ISOT — every model falls to somewhere between 57% and 61%, barely above chance. The in-distribution scores were never measuring detection; they were measuring familiarity with Reuters.

This is textbook shortcut learning: a model latches onto the simplest cue that correlates with the label, and when you remove it, it pivots to the next-simplest rather than to the intended task. Adversarial retraining neutralises the specific attack it is shown; it does not manufacture understanding. The root cause is not the architecture but the data — real and fake articles drawn from distinct sources will always leave a source-shaped shortcut for the model to take.

The way out

Three directions follow. Train on multiple, mixed sources so no single outlet's conventions can stand in for a class. Add a style-consistency objective that penalises a model for changing its verdict when only the surface style changes — though pushed too far, this risks erasing the genuine linguistic markers of deception too. And, most fundamentally, move beyond text classification toward evidence retrieval: systems that check an article's claims against an external knowledge base, because “unemployment is at 4.2%” versus “2.1%” cannot be told apart by style at all. Until detectors verify content rather than recognise registers, a 99% benchmark score should be read as a warning, not a result.
