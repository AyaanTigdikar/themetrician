The ocean's blind spot

We measure the sea's chemistry with exquisite precision and the people who use it barely at all. That imbalance leaves policymakers unable to see the disputes, and the opportunities, that socio-economic ocean data would reveal — and it is a market failure with a fixable shape.

Ayaan Tigdikar 2025 10 min read | Ocean data & governance

Adapted from a policy essay on global ocean data governance.

The argument in brief

  • Global ocean observing systems deliver more than a million measurements a day — but of the 50-plus Essential Ocean Variables, only three track human activity. The blue economy is dramatically under-measured.
  • Who needs the data and who can pay for it are mismatched: the communities most affected by ocean governance can least afford to fund data, while those who can afford it have little incentive to share. That is a textbook market failure.
  • Access follows sensitivity — from fisheries data that should be shared widely to military data that cannot be — which is exactly why socio-economic data lags scientific data.
  • The fix is coordination, not new science: an IOC-UNESCO-led architecture on FAIR principles, data-for-access licensing, and a dedicated fund pointed at the $175bn gap in the world's least-funded development goal.

Every day, satellites, autonomous gliders and research vessels return more than a million observations of the ocean. We can track its acidification, its oxygen, its warming with growing precision. What we cannot see, with anything like the same clarity, is us — the fishing fleets, shipping lanes, offshore rigs, and coastal communities whose activity is the actual subject of most ocean policy. The instruments are pointed at the water. The people using it stay in the dark.

The imbalance is baked into the categories themselves. Climate science has produced more than fifty Essential Ocean Variables — the standardised measurements that make forecasting and modelling possible. Of those, exactly three touch human activity at all: ocean colour, ocean sound, and marine debris. Everything else about how people use the sea is measured, if at all, in incompatible databases, project by project, country by country.

This is not for want of infrastructure. Physical oceanography leans on roughly 4,000 Argo floats returning 13,000 temperature and salinity profiles a month, with several hundred more measuring oxygen and pH. But that apparatus was built as an extension of national weather and defence systems, not as a global public good, and it was built largely by the United States and the European Union. When money flows — $101.5m to NOAA under the Inflation Reduction Act, €7.5m a year to Europe's EMODnet — it flows to the science. The blue economy, and the people in it, are left to fragmented, project-specific records that never add up to a picture a policymaker can use.

Who needs the data, and who can pay for it

To see why the gap persists, map the actors on two axes: how much they are willing to pay for ocean data, and how much they matter to the decisions the data should inform. The picture that emerges is not reassuring.

Two facts fall out of the grid. Those most affected by ocean governance — fishing and coastal communities, indigenous peoples, developing-nation governments — have the least capacity to fund data collection, even though their generations of traditional ecological knowledge about fish migration and ecosystem health are things no satellite can replicate. And those with the deepest pockets — governments and corporations — often have the strongest reasons not to share, because the data reveals something competitive or sensitive. Need and capacity sit in different corners of the box. That is the market failure, and it is the standard justification for public intervention.

The people who most need ocean data cannot pay for it; the people who can pay have every reason to keep it. Between those two corners sits a market failure.

From fisheries to warships

Not all ocean data is equally shareable, and that is the second reason the socio-economic side lags. Picture a spectrum of sensitivity. At one end sits data that everyone gains from pooling; at the other, data no state will ever hand over.

The left end is not hypothetical. Fisheries disputes — India and Sri Lanka in the Palk Bay, the UK and France after Brexit, the Philippines and China in the South China Sea — are in part information failures. Had officials shared systematic data on falling fish stocks, they could have seen the tensions coming and defused them before they escalated. In the middle sits deep-sea mining, thrown open by a 2025 US executive order and opposed by 32 countries calling for a moratorium: here the argument is that data on mineral deposits must be shared widely, precisely to prevent unilateral grabs of a common resource. At the right end are military vessel positions — more than 45 US freedom-of-navigation operations in the Indo-Pacific since 2017 — which cannot, and arguably should not, be shared at all. The lesson is that a workable system shares what it can and does not pretend the sensitive end away.

Proof that it can work

Scepticism about multilateral data-sharing is fair — until you look at the Sulu-Sulawesi seas. In 2017, Indonesia, Malaysia and the Philippines, facing a shared plague of piracy, kidnapping and trafficking, launched INDOMALPHI: coordinated patrols, joint command posts, and pooled intelligence. Piracy incidents in the patrol area fell from 99 in 2017 to zero by the first half of 2023.

What made it hold, even without a sanctions mechanism, is instructive: the three states faced identical threats and gained equally, technical expertise was built into the arrangement, compliance stayed high, and the politics aligned despite genuine territorial rivalries. Shared problem, shared benefit, credible expertise — that is the recipe, and it generalises far beyond piracy.

An architecture, not more instruments

The missing ingredient is coordination, not new technology. Four problems need solving at once: competing incentives across governments and firms; infrastructure costs beyond any single nation; the unfairness of data that is unavailable to those it most concerns, over an ocean that is 60% beyond any national jurisdiction; and the absence of any body to standardise human-activity data. One institution is already shaped for the job. IOC-UNESCO runs a network of more than 100 National Oceanographic Data Centres and a new multi-year strategy for ocean planning — the architecture is there to extend the discipline that produced the Essential Ocean Variables to the human side of the ledger.

The moving parts are unglamorous but well understood. Adopt FAIR principles — findable, accessible, interoperable, reusable — as the common technical standard rather than rigid compliance rules. Use data-for-access licensing: firms seeking to operate in a nation's waters contribute their data as a condition of the licence, turning regulatory leverage into a public good. Borrow a light-touch dispute forum on the model of the World Bank's ICSID, whose value is less enforcement than a common, credible reading of the facts. And pay for it through a dedicated Ocean Data Fund, financed by levies on ocean-dependent industries and pointed squarely at the $175bn gap in SDG 14. That multilateral funding is achievable is not a hope but a fact: the Global Ocean Observing System is already backed jointly by the US, China, the EU, Japan, India and others.

South Africa shows the payoff at national scale. Its OCIMS platform, built through a public-private partnership under Operation Phakisa, folds vessel tracking, aquaculture monitoring and spill detection into one system. A 2015 red tide cost the country's lobster industry $6.3m; with the monitoring tool now in place, authorities can act before the next one lands. Integrated data turns disasters into events you see coming.

Paying for it — and framing it

Some of the cheapest gains come from riding infrastructure that already exists. Submarine telecom cables can carry smart sensors along whole ocean basins; offshore wind platforms can host monitoring stations; commercial cargo ships can carry standard sensor packages that turn routine voyages into data-collection missions. The technology is not the constraint.

The real constraint is political: ocean-data infrastructure competes with housing and healthcare for budgets, and "ocean data" alone rarely wins that fight. The way through is framing — attaching the same monitoring to priorities that command money. Counter-trafficking and border security, climate compliance and marine carbon sinks, energy security and cable resilience, and the management of dangerous migration routes across the Mediterranean: each is a funding stream that a bare appeal to data cannot tap, and each is served by the very same sensors.

The upshot

Ocean data sits at an inflexion point. The instruments for measuring the sea's physics exist and keep improving; the equivalent effort on human activity barely exists at all. Without it, policymakers cannot see conflicts coming, cannot govern shared resources fairly, and cannot prevent damage they will later pay to repair. INDOMALPHI shows that collaboration works when the problem is clear and the gains are shared; the technology to scale it is already in the water; and the political framings that unlock the money are lying in plain sight. What is missing is the decision to point the apparatus, finally, at ourselves.
