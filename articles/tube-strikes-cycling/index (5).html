Missing the tube, finding the bike

Every London Tube strike is an experiment no commuter volunteered for. Using 4.7 million trips, two methods agree: strikes send about a third more riders to the city's bikes — and a small habit lingers for a week.

Ayaan Tigdikar202610 min read|Methods: event study · machine learning

Adapted from an LSE group project (ST446), 2026.

What the analysis finds

  • Across 4.7 million morning-peak trips, Tube strikes raise cycle-hire demand by about 36% on the strike day — large and precisely estimated.
  • Cycling stays jointly elevated for up to ten working days, but the day-to-day effects are noisy and driven by longer, warmer-month strikes.
  • A Random Forest using cycling behaviour alone (AUC 0.73) sees P(strike) jump from 0.34 to 0.54, then fade to 0.39–0.41 — a modest, short-lived habit.
  • The signature of a strike day is longer trips and more varied destinations, not simply higher volume. Disruption length is what turns a trial into a habit.

The London Underground carries over three million people on an ordinary day, and when its drivers walk out, all of them need another way to work. A natural experiment hides in those walkouts: each strike is a sudden, involuntary nudge that forces a slice of commuters to try something new. This study asks what happens when that something is a bike — and whether the habit sticks.

The setting is ideal because the economics is about forced experimentation. Commuters settle into routines that are good enough and stop searching, even when a better option exists, because the effort of trying is not worth it. A strike removes the choice: the Tube is simply gone, and some riders discover that London's cycle-hire scheme suits them. The question is whether they revert the moment service resumes, or keep pedalling. To answer it we build a station-day panel of roughly 4.7 million morning-peak cycle-hire trips from 2022 to 2025 and bring two complementary tools to bear: a causal event study and a machine-learning classifier.

What the strikes do

The first tool is a Negative Binomial event study: it compares cycling at each station in the days around a strike against that station's own baseline, absorbing weather, day-of-week and seasonal patterns. The design earns its keep in the flat pre-period — the days before a strike show no drift, which is what makes the strike-day jump interpretable as cause rather than coincidence.

On the strike day itself, cycling demand rises by about 36% — large, precisely estimated, and exactly what modal substitution predicts. Afterwards the picture is messier: demand stays elevated for up to ten working days and is jointly significant across the post-period, but the day-to-day estimates are imprecise, and a leave-one-out check shows the lasting bump is carried by particular strikes (the longer ones, and those in warm months) rather than being a uniform law. The habit is real but modest.

A strike is a free trial no commuter asked for. About a third more of them take the bike that day — and a few never quite give it back.

A second opinion from machine learning

The event study assumes a shape; the second tool does not. We train a Random Forest to recognise a strike day purely from cycling behaviour — trip durations, how many distinct destinations riders reach, distance to the nearest Tube station — with no calendar or disruption information at all. It then scores ordinary and post-strike days by how “strike-like” the cycling looks. Its modest skill (an AUC of 0.73) is itself informative: strike-day cycling has a detectable signature, but not an overwhelming one.

The classifier corroborates the event study from a different direction. Its strike-probability score jumps from a baseline of 0.34 to 0.54 on strike days, then settles to 0.39–0.41 for the following week — elevated, but much closer to normal than to a strike. Two methods, one conclusion: a big immediate shift, a small and fading residual.

What separates a strike day is not just volume but behaviour. Trip counts rise on many ordinary days too — good weather, events — so raw volume is a poor tell. The distinctive markers are longer rides and a wider spread of destinations, the fingerprint of people using bikes for journeys they would normally have made underground. A logistic-regression check flags the same features, which is reassuring: the pattern is a property of the data, not of one model.

The upshot for London

The two analyses agree. Tube strikes cause a substantial, immediate switch to cycling — roughly a third more trips — and a modest habit that lingers for about a working week before fading. That persistence is far weaker than the more-than-20% lasting rise other work has documented after the month-long COVID disruptions, which points to a simple lesson: the length of a disruption, not just its occurrence, is what turns a forced trial into a durable habit.

For Transport for London and for the bike-share operators, that is actionable. Strikes reliably deliver a wave of first-time riders; capturing even a fraction of them — through capacity, pricing, or a nudge to keep going — is where a short-run substitution could be converted into a long-run shift. Whether repeated short strikes eventually accumulate into that shift is the natural question for individual-level data to answer next.
