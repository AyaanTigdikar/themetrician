Vietnam has outgrown its roads

Three decades of miracle growth have carried Vietnam to the edge of middle income. A growth diagnostic finds the binding constraint on the next stage is neither finance nor institutions — it is the transport network the export economy has already outrun.

Ayaan Tigdikar 2025 11 min read | Method: growth diagnostics

Adapted from a Systematic Country Diagnostic of Vietnam (LSE School of Public Policy, PP413).

What the diagnostic finds

  • Vietnam has grown from a real GDP of $35bn in 1985 to $377bn in 2023 at roughly 6% a year — but that growth is increasingly carried by total factor productivity as the labour force ages and capital deepening stalls.
  • Walking the growth tree rules out the usual suspects: finance, macroeconomic risk, and weak institutions are not binding constraints in Vietnam today.
  • The binding constraint is transport infrastructure. Roads carry 84% of freight at a quality of 3.2 out of 7; logistics eat close to a fifth of GDP; and Vietnam sits below every regional peer — Cambodia included — on the logistics index.
  • The tell is in the cross-section: road-dependent sectors are far less profitable than the rest. The remedy is integrating and digitising a multimodal network, not simply laying more tarmac.

In 1975, as the last American helicopters lifted from the embassy roof in Saigon, Vietnam was a nation in ruins — its infrastructure shattered, its economy reduced to subsistence farming. Few would have guessed that within a few decades it would become one of the fastest-growing economies on earth. The 1986 Đổi Mới reforms swapped central planning for a market-first model, opened the door to foreign investment, and set off an ascent that has lifted 30 million people out of poverty.

The numbers are still striking. Real GDP has risen roughly elevenfold since the mid-1980s, growth has averaged around 6% a year, and — unusually — it has been among the least volatile in the region, staying positive even through the strictest COVID lockdowns. Exports now run at about 87% of GDP, a share bettered only by Singapore.

But an ascent is not a guarantee. Vietnam has aged out of its demographic window: the population has crossed 100 million, the share over 65 is climbing, and the labour force is beginning to shrink. Gross capital formation has slipped from 32% of GDP to around 23% and stalled there, so capital deepening is slowing just as the workforce plateaus. With neither more workers nor much more capital to draw on, the growth that remains is being carried by total factor productivity — doing more with what you have.

That reframes the question. It is no longer how fast Vietnam grows, but how it sustains the growth it has once demography and capital stop doing the work. Answering that means finding what, at the margin, is holding productive activity back.

Reading the growth tree

The tool for that job is growth diagnostics: rather than hand a country a wish-list of every reform in the textbook, it works down a decision tree to isolate the one constraint that binds hardest — the reform that would buy the most growth per unit of effort. A constraint is binding if its shadow price is high, if the people affected are visibly working around it, and if the sectors that depend on it most are the ones underperforming.

Applied to Vietnam, most of the usual candidates fall away. Access to finance is not binding: credit is widely available, real interest rates sit within regional norms, savings are high, and finance-heavy sectors are not starved — when a large bank wobbled, the state stepped in with liquidity worth around 6% of GDP. Macroeconomic risk is not binding either: public debt is just 33.8% of GDP, against 65% in Thailand, 68% in Malaysia and 175% in Singapore, and the current account runs a healthy surplus. And appropriation is not binding: while corruption exists, fewer than 5% of firms name it their biggest obstacle, and licences take about eleven days — better than most peers. What firms do complain about, again and again, is moving their goods.

An export economy, strangled by its logistics

Vietnam exports the equivalent of 87% of its GDP, mostly electronics and textiles that have to travel from inland factories to coastal ports. That makes the transport network not a convenience but the load-bearing wall of the whole growth model — and it is cracking. Almost everything moves by road: some 84% of freight, with waterways a distant second and rail and air negligible.

For a country this dependent on trade, the logistics scorecard is alarming. Vietnam ranks below every one of its peers on the World Bank's Logistics Performance Index — including Cambodia, an economy a tenth its size — and logistics costs have climbed back toward a staggering 19% of GDP, among the highest in the region.

The roads that can't keep up

Vietnam has more than 570,000 km of road, but only 816 km of expressway, and the quality is poor: a rating of 3.2 out of 7 that sits well below what a country of its income and export volume should manage. Benchmarked against peers, Vietnam falls clearly beneath the trend line linking road quality to income — down with the Philippines, far from Singapore and Malaysia.

The cost of that shortfall is paid in time. A ten-kilometre commute takes over half an hour in Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City — a shadow price on movement rivalled in the region only by Manila, a city synonymous with gridlock. Every hour a truck idles in traffic is an hour of output that does not happen, and a worker who arrives exhausted.

The clinching evidence for a binding constraint is not the average but the cross-section. Sectors that lean heavily on road transport are markedly less profitable than sectors that don't — precisely the fingerprint the growth-diagnostics framework looks for. When a factor is scarce, the activities that need it most are the ones that suffer.

When a factor is scarce, the activities that lean on it hardest are the ones that suffer — and in Vietnam, the road-dependent sectors are exactly the laggards.

And the ports behind the roads

Roads are only half the story. Because Vietnam lives by exporting, its seaports are the final gate every container must pass, and they are congested — Cat Lai and Hai Phong run above 90% capacity — and under-invested in the deep-water terminals and smart-port technology that would clear them. Port quality, like road quality, sits below the trend its income implies.

The consequence is delay, and delay is expensive when your comparative advantage is being a reliable link in somebody else's supply chain. Vietnamese exports sit at the docks for longer than those of most comparators — the kind of friction that quietly erodes the country's pitch to the manufacturers it wants to attract.

The syndrome: outgrowing the network

Put together, the pieces name a syndrome: Vietnam has outgrown its transport infrastructure. Thirty years of miracle growth were never matched by the roads and ports to carry it — and, tellingly, the gap has a political-economy root. As a socialist state, Vietnam prioritised spreading investment evenly across regions to narrow the gap between rich and poor provinces. Laudable in itself, that impulse produced low-return regional ports, cabotage rules that shield domestic shippers and push more freight onto the roads, and highways in secondary cities that sit half-used — while the export corridors around Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City choke. Equity, pursued through the investment budget, came at the expense of the growth that funds it.

The encouraging part is that this is a constraint the state has both the capacity and the will to relax, and is beginning to. In 2024 the government committed some $27bn in public investment, with roughly two-thirds earmarked for road infrastructure — a signal that the binding constraint is understood, even if the response is still weighted toward pouring more concrete.

The fix is integration, not just tarmac

The instinct to build more is understandable, but the diagnostic points somewhere subtler. Vietnam's problem is less a shortage of infrastructure than the poor use of what exists — the same lesson the Growth Lab drew from Chiapas in Mexico, where the constraint was utilisation, not quantity. Roads carry 84% of freight not because the alternatives are impossible but because waterways, rail and ports have never been knitted into a network that lets a container change modes without friction.

Two moves follow. The first is to integrate a genuinely multimodal network through public-private partnerships — connecting existing and planned road, rail, inland-waterway and port links so freight flows to the cheapest mode rather than defaulting to the truck. The second is to digitise the logistics chain: smart-port systems, customs digitisation and freight coordination that cut the dwell times and the paperwork delays which today add cost at every handoff. Together they promise to relieve congestion and drain the logistics bill without waiting a decade for concrete to cure.

The upshot

Vietnam's growth story has entered a new chapter. The tailwinds that powered the first three decades — a young workforce, rapid capital accumulation, the one-off gains of opening up — are fading, and what remains depends on using the economy's capacity more productively. The single largest obstacle to that is the network on which an export economy lives. Fix the movement of goods, and the productivity growth Vietnam now relies on has room to run; leave it, and the miracle meets the limit of its own roads.
